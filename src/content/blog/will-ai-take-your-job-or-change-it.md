---
title: "Will AI take your job or change it?"
description: "Artificial intelligence is removing some tasks, creating others and pushing employers to rethink what a job actually contains."
date: 2026-11-27T09:45:00Z
tags: ["AI"]
---

"Will AI take my job?" is an understandable question, and a slightly misleading one. Jobs are bundles of tasks. A teacher explains ideas, plans lessons, manages a classroom, tracks progress and encourages students. A nurse records information, coordinates care, notices small changes in a patient and comforts families. AI may handle part of a job without replacing the whole role.

That doesn't make the disruption harmless. When enough tasks are automated, employers may hire fewer people, merge positions or demand more output from smaller teams. Entry-level workers may be most exposed, because their jobs often include routine research, drafting, data entry or basic coding.

## The evidence is mixed for a reason

Companies have announced layoffs and pointed to AI and efficiency. The outplacement firm Challenger, Gray & Christmas counted nearly 55,000 US job cuts in 2025 that employers directly blamed on AI. That's a real number, and it was also about 4.5% of all announced cuts that year.

At the same time, some employers have rehired workers after finding that automation needed more supervision than expected or couldn't replace what experienced staff knew. Klarna began hiring human customer-service agents again in 2025, and Ford said in 2026 that it had hired or promoted more than 350 veteran engineers after AI quality-control tools fell short. The Associated Press noted that nobody knows yet how common these reversals are.

Both stories can be true. AI does some tasks very well and others poorly. Its effect depends on the occupation, the employer, how well the company implements it and the state of the economy. A company may blame AI for a decision, such as layoffs, when the real reasons are partly about factors such as cost cutting or weak demand. Take a company that cuts 1,000 jobs and says, "AI now does much of this work." That sounds modern and forward-looking. The fuller story might be that sales are down (weak demand) or the company wants to spend less (cost cutting), and it would have cut those jobs anyway. "AI" makes a better headline for investors and the public than "business is slow." So when a company credits AI for job cuts, it's fair to check whether its sales or profits were also falling.

Employer expectations point both ways too. In McKinsey's 2026 State of AI survey, 39% of respondents expected AI to reduce their organization's headcount in the coming year, up from 32% a year earlier. Only 14% said AI had actually contributed to a smaller workforce in the past year.

## Work may intensify even when jobs remain

AI can make a worker more productive, but employers may turn every saved minute into a higher target. Employees can face pressure to watch over several agents, fix their errors and keep learning tools that change constantly. If a company measures performance only by volume, faster technology may produce more stress and worse work.

There's also a risk of deskilling. When a system handles every first draft or routine case, workers lose chances to build basic expertise. Employers need training paths that let people practice judgment, so their work involves more than approving a machine's output.

## Preparing without predicting the impossible

No one can name every job that will exist ten years from now. Workers can still prepare by building skills that AI complements: deep knowledge of their field, communication, critical thinking, relationships with people, and responsibility for high-stakes decisions. AI literacy, which means knowing how to use, question and check these tools, will matter in many occupations.

Employers and governments have obligations too. Training can't fall entirely on the people whose jobs are already at risk. Support during job changes, benefits that move with workers, clear workplace policies and investment in new career paths will help decide whether AI's gains reach most people.

People will shape the future of work through choices about who owns the technology, who gets its benefits, how workers take part in redesigning their jobs, and whether higher productivity serves people as well as company balance sheets.
