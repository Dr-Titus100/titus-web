An employee has a deadline. The company's approved tools are slow or limited, so the employee opens a public AI service, pastes in a document and gets an answer. The work gets done, and sensitive information may have left the company's control.

This is shadow AI: the use of AI tools without formal approval or oversight. A related habit, sometimes called shadow vibe coding, happens when employees use plain-language coding tools to build apps or automations outside the normal IT process. ("Vibe coding" means describing what you want to an AI and letting it write the code.)

## Why people go around official systems

Most employees aren't trying to cause harm. They want to solve a problem. Public tools may be easier to use than the approved ones, and a worker may not realize that a customer list, a contract or internal code is sensitive.

AI also makes building software feel deceptively simple. A person describes an expense tracker or a reporting dashboard, and a coding agent produces something that seems to work. That speed is valuable, especially when the IT department has a long backlog.

## The risks stay hidden until they surface

A public AI service may keep information under terms the employee never read. Generated code can contain security flaws or components used without the right license. An unofficial app may calculate figures wrongly, give too many people access or rely on a service that later disappears.

The scale of the problem is starting to show. In 2026, the security firm Red Access scanned more than 380,000 publicly reachable apps built on popular vibe-coding platforms such as Lovable, Replit and Base44. About 5,000 appeared to belong to companies, and more than 2,000 of those exposed sensitive data with no login or access controls at all. IBM's 2025 Cost of a Data Breach report found that 63% of breached organizations had no policy for governing AI.

The organization may not even know a tool exists. If the person who built it leaves, nobody may understand how an important workflow runs. It's the AI version of the forgotten spreadsheet that quietly runs a department, except this one may connect to cloud systems and take actions on its own.

## Bans are rarely enough

A blanket ban can push use further underground. Organizations need a practical alternative: approved tools that meet real needs, plain-language rules about what data people may share, and a fast review path for apps that employees build.

Workers should be able to experiment safely in a sandbox with fake or anonymized data. Higher-risk uses need stronger review. These include financial decisions, health information, employee evaluations, messages to customers and access to live production systems.

Leaders should also ask why shadow tools are attractive. They may reveal real bottlenecks that official systems never fixed. The employee who builds a rough AI workflow may have found an important opportunity, even if the first version is unsafe.

Shadow AI is a sign that powerful technology has become easier to get than organizational permission. Treating it only as a discipline problem misses that. The best response combines useful tools, clear limits, training and a path that turns good employee experiments into secure, supported systems.
